A resume does not mention a skill. Does that mean the person does not have it?

No. It means the available document does not establish it. That distinction becomes important when an AI turns incomplete information into scores, recommendations, or decisions.

In a long job-matching project conversation, the design evolved toward separate match, partial-match, non-match, and unknown states. It also distinguished evidence from a resume from information the owner later supplied. Some of those changes remained plans in the reviewed session, so this article explains the design rather than claiming a fully validated matching system.

A binary answer can hide three situations

Suppose a role asks for experience with a database. The candidate's information may explicitly establish that experience, explicitly establish that they do not have it, or say nothing about it.

Those are different evidence states. Reducing them to yes and no forces missing information into one of the other categories.

Guessing yes creates unsupported claims. Guessing no can discard a suitable option. Keeping unknown lets the product ask for clarification or explain the limit of its analysis.

The same distinction applies to apartment preferences, customer records, document extraction, and many other products built on incomplete input. Absence from the source is not automatically evidence of absence in the world.

Define each label with examples

Labels such as partial can be vague unless the team agrees what they mean. A partial match might mean that some requirements are supported, or that a related skill provides incomplete evidence for the exact requirement.

Write examples that distinguish the categories. A fictional request for three years of experience might be supported, contradicted, or unresolved depending on the dates and wording available.

Do not let the model invent the category definitions during each run. The product should provide the rule, and evaluation should check whether the model applies it consistently. Otherwise, the same label can mean different things across items that users are trying to compare.

Keep the source of a claim

A claim may come from a document, a direct user answer, or an inference. Those sources can all be useful, but they support different levels of confidence and verification.

For each important result, preserve enough provenance to explain where it came from. Provenance is simply the origin and history of the information.

An owner-provided answer should not silently become a resume-verified fact. Likewise, an inferred relationship between two technologies should not look like explicit experience with both. The interface can summarize the distinction in ordinary language without forcing the user to read internal model reasoning.

Ask for information that changes the decision

Unknown fields can generate too many questions if the product asks about every missing detail. Prioritize the questions that materially affect the recommendation.

For example, whether a candidate can work in the required location may matter more than a minor preference when deciding which roles to show first. The priority depends on the user's goals and the product's actual rules.

Bundle related questions where that reduces effort, and preserve answers so the person does not have to repeat them. A clarification loop should make the next result better, not become a recurring intake form that forgets what it already learned.

Treat answers as versioned evidence

When a person adds information, the system's knowledge changes. Results calculated from the earlier profile may now be incomplete or wrong.

Record which profile version produced a result and decide which results need reconsideration. The product can then explain that an older recommendation used an earlier set of facts.

This does not require recalculating every historical item immediately. Prioritize useful items and keep resource limits explicit. The important point is that new evidence has a defined effect rather than leaving the system with a mixture of unexplained old and new answers.

Avoid confidence scores that conceal missing information

A model can sound certain even when its input is incomplete. A number such as 92% confidence does not solve the evidence problem by itself.

Ask what the confidence refers to. Is it confidence that the text says something, that the interpretation is correct, or that the real-world person meets the requirement? Those are different claims.

An evidence label and a short explanation may be more useful than an unexplained numerical score. If the product uses confidence to control behavior, evaluate that relationship against observed correctness. Do not assume a self-reported score is a calibrated probability.

Let the user correct the interpretation

A result should provide a practical route to correction. If the system says a requirement is unknown, the user may supply the missing detail. If it misread a document, the user should be able to identify the incorrect interpretation.

Keep the correction connected to the evidence and result it changes. Otherwise, an edit may improve one screen while a cached ranking continues using the old assumption.

The product should also distinguish a factual correction from a preference change. Both can affect recommendations, but one changes what is known and the other changes what the person wants. That distinction helps explain later differences.

Evaluate the categories separately

An overall accuracy score can hide a system that rarely admits uncertainty. Measure whether supported claims, contradictions, and missing evidence are each handled appropriately.

Include examples where the tempting answer is plausible but unsupported. Include explicit negative evidence so unknown does not become an escape hatch for every difficult case.

Have a person inspect disagreements using the same category definitions. If reviewers cannot agree, the policy may need clarification before more prompting work will help. The answer sheet needs to express a stable product rule, not simply mirror the model's latest output.

Keep ranking separate from truth

A product may rank an item lower because important information is missing. That can be a reasonable decision, but it should not relabel the missing fact as false.

Explain the ranking effect separately: the item remains uncertain until a key requirement is clarified. A user may choose to explore it anyway.

This preserves flexibility. Different users may have different tolerance for uncertainty, while the underlying evidence should remain the same. Mixing policy with factual labels makes it harder to change the ranking strategy without rewriting the apparent truth of every result.

Preserve the distinction in exports

The distinction must survive beyond the visible screen. An exported file or downstream API should not collapse unknown into a blank value that another system interprets as false. Include an explicit category and, where appropriate, the evidence supporting it.

Otherwise, careful product reasoning disappears at the next boundary. A readable badge helps the current user, while a stable data contract helps every later consumer interpret the result correctly. The same example should mean the same thing in the interface, the exported record, and the evaluation used to check the model.

Make uncertainty useful

The objective is not to fill the product with disclaimers. It is to identify the specific information that would improve a decision and provide a manageable next step.

An honest result can be concise: the available document supports two requirements, contradicts one, and leaves one unresolved. The user then understands what is known and what would change the assessment.

That is a strong product and leadership principle for AI systems. Useful intelligence includes recognizing the boundary of the available evidence and helping the person move that boundary with the least unnecessary effort.

Technical deep dive

Plain-language bridge: Missing support and explicit contradiction are different states. The data model needs enough room to preserve both.

These categories describe evidence, not a numerical confidence score. A ranking policy may penalize missing information without turning it into an asserted falsehood.

Represent evidence before deriving a label

An illustrative assessment separates evidence from the decision applied to it:

{
  "requirementId": "example-skill",
  "evidenceState": "missing",
  "supportingRefs": [],
  "contradictingRefs": [],
  "assessmentVersion": 3,
  "policyVersion": 2
}

An evidence reference should identify the source version and relevant passage or fact. A derived category such as partial match may combine several requirements; it should not erase the underlying evidence states.

Do not automatically treat absence of support as contradiction. Also define what happens when both supporting and contradicting evidence exist. A conflict can require review instead of being collapsed into whichever source was processed last.

Keep logic and ranking separate

For a requirement with states supported, contradicted, missing, and conflicting, write a decision table before implementing aggregation. For example, a mandatory requirement with missing evidence may produce "needs clarification," while an explicit contradiction may produce "does not currently meet the rule."

Ranking is a policy applied afterward. It may penalize unresolved requirements, but the penalty should not relabel the evidence. Different ranking policies can then operate on the same factual assessment without rewriting its provenance.

Avoid generic Boolean coercion such as treating an absent field as false. Validation at every serialization boundary should preserve the allowed categories, including exports.

Evaluate abstention as a product behavior

Coverage is the fraction of cases where the system issues a definitive answer. Conditional error is the error rate among those definitive answers. Lowering coverage can reduce conditional error by abstaining more often, so report both.

Also inspect inappropriate abstention: cases with enough evidence where the system still says unknown. A product that avoids every mistake by answering nothing is rarely useful. Break results down by missing evidence, explicit contradiction, supported facts, and conflicts instead of hiding the differences in one accuracy number.

Test evidence updates and reversals

Add a new source that resolves a missing requirement and verify that the assessment changes only where its dependencies changed. Then retract or supersede that source and confirm the result can return to unknown. "Once supported, always supported" is not a safe default when evidence has a lifecycle.

Preserve the version used for earlier decisions so a later explanation can distinguish a changed fact from a changed policy. These are proposed implementation details for the historical design direction, not proof of a completed recommendation system.
